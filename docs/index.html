Fix First

13,562 pages · 29 clients · 9 held out

A content team can review about fifty pages a month out of thousands. So the useful question is not "which pages are dropping" but "which fifty do we look at first". I wrote a five-line rule, then trained four models to beat it, and tested every one on companies none of them had seen. The best model did not beat the rule by more than luck can explain.

Refresh-priority ranking on 30,000 pseudonymised pages from 32 clients, trailing 90-day search metrics. A hand-written rule against four scikit-learn models, all scored on clients held out whole with GroupShuffleSplit. Primary metric Precision@50, with a cluster bootstrap over held-out clients, because only the top of the queue is ever acted on.

Why the rule ships

If two tools do the same job, pick the one you can read. The rule explains every page it picks in one sentence, and nobody has to trust it. The model has to earn its place over that, and on this data it did not.

The rule's own Precision@50 moves between 0.76 and 0.92 purely from how tied scores are broken, and the model's +0.02 sits inside that band. Across 8 grouped splits the model led in 6, mean margin 0.056: directionally positive, not demonstrated. Equivalent performance plus free interpretability means the rule ships.

02The question

Which fifty pages should a team look at first?

A ranking problem under a fixed review budget, not a classification problem

FlyRank's clients publish more pages than anyone can keep up to date. Pages age, search results shift, and traffic drifts down too slowly for anyone to notice one specific page dying. The limit is not knowledge. It is hours.

So this project answers a narrow question on purpose: with a fixed number of review hours, which pages go to the top of the pile? It does not ask whether rewriting a page brings its traffic back. Nothing in this data can answer that, because nobody ever tested it fairly. Section 07 is what happened when I tried anyway.

The target is the dataset's is_declining label. Only the head of the ranking is ever consumed, so the primary metric is Precision@K at a realistic review budget (K = 50), with ROC-AUC as a secondary whole-ranking check.

The data is one cross-sectional snapshot with no intervention, so every output here is decision support: a better order to review in. Nothing on this page is a claim that refreshing a page changes its traffic.

That leaves 13,562 pages across 29 clients. It means everything here describes pages that already get found. Pages nobody finds are a different problem.

03The rule to beat

Five lines anyone can read

A two-term heuristic, written before any model and kept as the bar

The rule gives a page points for two things: getting fewer clicks than other pages at the same search position, and not being updated for a long time. Add them up, sort, done.

CTR shortfall against the median of the page's own position tier, plus a capped staleness term. No fitting, no parameters learned from the label. 

tier_median   = median CTR of all pages in the same position tier
ctr_gap_ratio = clip((tier_median - ctr) / tier_median, 0, 1)
freshness     = min(days_since_last_update / 365, 1)
score         = ctr_gap_ratio + freshness

Its blind spot is visible in the arithmetic

A page with zero clicks gets the biggest possible click gap, so pages nobody clicks float straight to the top. The models learned the same habit. Section 06 is what those pages turned out to be.

04Unseen clients

How you test mattered more than which model

Row split vs client split: +0.080 ROC-AUC, 8 of 8 draws

Pages from one company share a website, an audience and a tracking setup. If some of a company's pages are in training and others are in the test, the model can learn "this company's pages tend to drop" and score well without learning anything about pages at all.

Rows are not independent within a client. A row-level split lets the model memorise client-level base rates and score them as skill. The fix is GroupShuffleSplit on client_id, 30% of clients held out whole, and asserted in code rather than assumed.

0.728

ROC-AUC when pages are split at random, so companies appear on both sides

0.618

Same model, same data, with whole companies held out

Across 8 random draws the careless split scored higher 8 times out of 8, by +0.080 on average. That is bigger than the gap between any two models I tried. Someone who split by row would report a better result in good faith, having done nothing a tutorial warned them about. 

assert not (set(train_clients) & set(test_clients)), "clients leaked across the split"
05The leak nobody warns about

The answer was hiding in two ordinary columns

The label is exactly reconstructible from two unbanned columns

The dataset's notes warn you off two columns, because the "is this page dropping" answer is made from them. Ban those two and you are told you are safe. You are not. Those columns are themselves made from two more columns that are still sitting in the table, with harmless-looking names.

The documentation bans trend_direction and trend_pct. But trend_pct is computed from the 30-day impression windows, which remain in the table under ordinary names. One line of arithmetic reconstructs it: 

trend_pct == (impressions_last_30d - impressions_prev_30d) / impressions_prev_30d * 100

correlation 1.0000, rows matching 26,612 of 26,612

Add those two columns to the model and held-out ROC-AUC jumps from 0.618 to 0.992. That looks like a triumph and is worth nothing. The model was handed the answer in two pieces and asked to do the subtraction. It is a bigger effect than the split problem, +0.374 against +0.080, and harder to catch.

Then I stopped guessing and tested every column

Finding that by reasoning was luck. So I added every remaining column to the clean model one at a time and watched the score. I also slipped the banned answer column in on purpose. If the test could not catch the leak I already knew about, its silence on the others would mean nothing.

Every column added singly to the clean feature set, same client-grouped split, with trend_pct and trend_direction as planted positive controls. Both controls lift ROC-AUC by +0.382, so the instrument works.

It found a column I had missed, clicks_last_30d (+0.088). All four worst offenders are last_30d and prev_30d columns. Nothing is special about any one name: they leak because the label's measuring period lives inside them.

The rule I carry to the next project

Banning the label is not enough, and a list of banned names is a patch for one dataset. Ban anything measured over the same window the label is measured over. That sentence travels.

06The zero-click pages

Nearly half the top 50 was not a content problem

24 of the top 50 are pages with impressions and zero clicks

Of the held-out pages, 388 of 2,955 show up in search but record no clicks at all. They hold 24 of the top 50 queue slots, because zero clicks gives the biggest possible click gap. Rewriting a page cannot fix that if the real problem is somewhere else.

The easy guesses are broken tracking, bots, or an answer that shows right in Google so nobody clicks. I tested instead of listing. Every one of the 2,045 zero-click pages in the eligible set still gets visits, a median of 5. So people do reach them.

Clicks and sessions come from two systems: Search Console counts search clicks, analytics counts arrivals. Of 2,045 eligible pages with zero search clicks in 90 days, every one records sessions (median 5, up to 572), and no page anywhere has clicks without sessions.

  • Not AI referrals. Only 2.9% of zero-click pages get any AI visit, against 8.6% of pages that do get clicks.
  • Not pages that push people away. They turn views into visits better, not worse: 7.43 visits per 1,000 views against 5.80.
  • Shaped by company. Among clients with 50 or more eligible pages, the zero-click rate runs from 4% to 55%. A page-quality problem would look similar everywhere. A spread that wide looks like a setup difference.

So the queue's first rule is a refusal

Zero-click pages are blocked: check tracking first, do not send a writer. The data has no traffic-channel breakdown, so I cannot prove where the visits came from. But two of the three explanations are now ruled out by measurement, not by hunch.

07The test that failed

Optimized pages recovered. The data cannot say why.

Difference-in-differences on 13,233 optimized pages fails its placebo

FlyRank's bigger data warehouse, 79 million rows, records the date real pages were really optimized. That is close to an experiment. So I compared pages optimized in May 2026 with pages from the same companies that were never touched, over the same days.

The gated warehouse release carries dim_content.last_optimized_date. Treated: 13,233 eligible pages optimized in May 2026, the only month with a full 30 days either side. Control: never-optimized pages from the same clients over the same calendar days. Intervals from 2,000 resamples of whole clients.

EstimateClicks per page, 30 days95% interval
Before vs after, naive+1.84 
Matched on size, within client+2.75+1.76 to +3.50
Placebo: two windows, both before−0.71−1.21 to −0.11

+2.75 clicks a page looked like a result. I believed it for about ten minutes. Then I ran the same comparison on two stretches of time that were both before anyone touched the pages. That should come out at zero. It came out at −0.71: the optimized pages were already falling before anyone changed them.

The placebo on two pre-treatment windows should be zero if parallel trends hold. It is −0.71 (p = 0.026), with an interval entirely below zero, so the assumption the design rests on is violated. The matched interval also excludes zero, and that rescues nothing: a tight interval around a biased estimate is still centred on the wrong number.

That shape is a V. The optimized pages dipped, then came back to 97% of their own starting level: back to normal, not above it. Two stories fit. Either the optimization worked, or teams picked pages during a bad month and bad months end. Only the second predicts a return to baseline rather than past it.

What would settle it, for free

Take next month's list, optimize a random half, leave the rest alone for 30 days. Choosing at random makes the two groups comparable by design, and the placebo would come back at zero.

08What a team gets

A queue with a reason on every page

Archetypes, review gates and a no-go list, not a bare score

A score of 0.93 is not something a writer can act on. So every page in the queue gets a plain pattern a person can confirm in a minute, mapped to one action.

PatternPagesAction

Before anyone touches a page, four checks: tracking is alive, demand is real (500+ views), the position is known, and the click gap is measurable. Of the 2,955 held-out pages, 943 clear all four and go to a writer, 1,624 need an analyst first, and 388 are blocked.

What should never be automated

  • Publishing a rewrite nobody read. The ranking never reads the page.
  • Deleting or de-indexing anything. Nothing here measures a page's value away from search.
  • Ranking one client against another. Their tracking setups differ; scores only compare inside one client.
  • Promising a traffic outcome. Section 07 is why.

The queue is worth most at the top. It is right 88% of the time in the top 50 and 73.5% by the top 200, so it is a triage aid, not a list to work through to the end.

Precision decays with depth: 0.88 at K = 50, 0.735 at K = 200, against a 0.551 base rate. Retrain quarterly, not monthly: the label is cut from a 90-day window, so monthly retrains would mostly re-learn the same rows.

09What it cannot tell you

Nine companies, one snapshot, no experiment

Limits, and the ones I tested rather than just admitted

  • Nine held-out clients. Enough to expose the split problem, too few to say how the ranking behaves across every industry FlyRank serves. That is why the model's own interval runs from 0.60 to 0.92.
  • One snapshot, nobody assigned. This can say "look at these first". It can never say "rewriting this will bring traffic back".
  • The label is the dataset's definition of dropping, not an independent measure of harm to the business.
  • The size gate, tested. I re-ran everything at gates from 100 to 2,000 views, each over 8 client splits. The pool halves, from 15,091 pages to 7,730, and the model's edge stays between +0.027 and +0.087 while the rule still wins some splits at every gate. The tie is a property of the problem, not of my threshold.
  • Content decay is not measurable here. I expected older pages to drop more. But the "last updated" column has a median of 3 distinct values per client: it acts like a per-company crawl date, not a per-page edit date. So half of my own rule's freshness term carries almost no signal. I report that rather than a decay chart.
  • The leak test only catches what moves the score. A column that leaks only in combination with another would pass it. A flag is strong evidence; no flag is weaker.
10Reproduce it

Run it yourself

capstone.ipynb is the single source of truth. It runs top to bottom against the committed data in about two and a half minutes on a laptop, and ends with assertions that fail the run rather than print a warning: no client on both sides of the split, the leak test still catches a leak, and the model-versus-rule interval still contains zero. 

git clone https://github.com/Zeref538/flyrank-ml-internship
cd flyrank-ml-internship
pip install -r requirements.txt nbconvert ipykernel
jupyter nbconvert --to notebook --execute --inplace work/notebooks/capstone.ipynb

The warehouse study, w08_warehouse_did.ipynb, also needs gated access to the warehouse release. Every number on this page is rebuilt from the committed result files by docs/build_case_study.py, which refuses to build if the prose disagrees with them.
